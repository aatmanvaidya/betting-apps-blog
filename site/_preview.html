Betting apps find Indian users through a steady stream of Facebook and Instagram ads that promise quick, risk-free money. We read 10,526 of those ads, 2,695 Instagram posts and 329,501 one-star app reviews, and compared the pitch with what people say happens after they sign up.

By Aatman Vaidya (University of Tübingen) & Kiran Garimella (Rutgers University)

10,526betting ads from Meta’s Ad Library, coded by hand
2,695public Instagram posts from 10 betting hashtags
3.3 lakhone-star Google Play reviews of 58 apps

If you use Instagram in India, you have probably seen one. A cricketer you recognise promising that your money will multiply. A crash-game multiplier climbing to 2.72x. A screenshot of a winning slip with a WhatsApp number underneath.

Online betting is a multi-billion-dollar industry, and in India it sits in a legal grey zone. Gambling is regulated state by state. Many platforms call themselves games of skill. Offshore operators that get blocked come back under new domains and new brand names. News reports and public-health researchers have tied these apps to financial loss, debt, addiction and, in some cases, loss of life. A 2025 law aims to curb real-money gaming ads.

For most people, the first contact with a betting app is not an app store. It is an ad in their feed. So we started with recruitment: what the ads promise, how the same promise travels on Instagram, and how it compares with what users report once they have deposited their money.

The pitchप्रचार

Nearly every ad sold easy money

One of us went through all 10,526 ads by hand, coding what each one sells, how it tries to persuade, and which potentially harmful narratives it carries. Near-identical creatives were grouped first, so each unique ad was read once. The codebook grew out of the ads themselves.

Two narratives ran through almost the entire corpus. The ads were also built for conversion: 99.8% were aimed at signing up new users, 85.9% sold a specific feature such as an instant withdrawal or a sign-up bonus, and 59.1% leaned on social proof like winning slips and screenshots of payouts.

99.4%Easy money. Small stakes shown turning into large, quick wins.
98.4%Risk minimization. Outcomes framed as safe, knowable or verified.
67.4%Misleading or deceptive claims, by our coding.

Synthetic credibilityउधार के चेहरे

Borrowed faces

Without a friend’s share or a creator’s following to vouch for them, paid ads manufacture trust: fake news bulletins, cloned voices, celebrities who never said a word.

The game being soldप्लेन हमेशा उड़ जाता है

The plane always flies away

Aviator is a crash game. A multiplier climbs from 1.00x as a small plane takes off. Cash out before it flies away and your stake is multiplied. Wait too long and the stake is gone. Rounds last seconds, so a player can bet hundreds of times an hour.

The ads sell it as something you can read. “Predictor” apps promise to call the crash from the strip of past results at the top of the screen. Telegram channels sell signals. A testimonial video shows a running profit counter.

Simulated rounds, random, 3% house edge

Each round is an independent draw. The strip of past results that predictor apps claim to read says nothing about the next one.

Crash games advertise a 97% “return to player.” That means that across all bets, the house keeps about three rupees in every hundred. It does this every round, whatever the player does.

One ad in our data turned the house’s cut into a slogan: “97% players win every day.”

Try every strategy

2.00xwins 48.5% of rounds
1,000 simulated players each start with ₹10,000 and bet ₹100 a round for 200 rounds, cashing out at the same target every time.

No cash-out target beats the 3% edge. Low targets win often and bleed slowly. High targets lose most rounds and occasionally pay out big. Those rare big wins are real, and they are exactly the screenshots that end up in ads and on Instagram.

Burn and churnआज यहाँ, कल ग़ायब

Here today, gone in four days

Betting ads live fast. Among the betting ads with a recorded end date, the median ran for four days. 43% ran three days or fewer, and two in three were gone within a week.

How long each betting ad ran

Days between first and last delivery. 8,142 distinct ads with a recorded end date.

New betting ads by the month they started

The Ad Library keeps recent ads more reliably than old ones, so part of the rise reflects what it retains. Collection ended in July 2025.

Short runs suit a simple playbook. Make a creative, launch it from a fresh page, collect sign-ups for a few days, abandon the page before enforcement catches up. The betting ads came from 1,224 different Facebook pages, and generative AI has pushed the cost of a new creative close to zero.

Enforcement does happen, eventually. By the time we collected the data, Meta had already taken down 20,591 ads in our keyword search. But during the lag, the platform keeps the ad revenue and the advertiser keeps the sign-ups. Meta’s own policies prohibit real-money gambling ads without prior written permission.

There is also a transparency gap. For these ads in India, the Ad Library shows no reach, spend, payer or beneficiary. The same kind of ad in the European Union has to disclose who paid for it and who saw it.

Instagram · 10 hashtagsक्रिकेट की भाषा

On Instagram, the pitch speaks cricket

Hashtags show what circulates outside the ad system. We collected 2,695 public posts from ten betting hashtags, and Gemini 2.5 Pro coded the 2,607 relevant ones using our codebook and hand-labelled examples. On 300 held-out posts it agreed with our labels 97% of the time.

The organic layer is blunter than the ads. 71.9% of posts were about sports betting, overwhelmingly cricket. It leans on celebrities, emotion and winners’ stories, and says out loud what paid ads avoid.

A random sample of posts from each hashtag; posts flagged as sexual content are excluded. Tap a post to see how it was coded.

A distribution network dressed as fan pages

We reviewed about 200 posts and the profiles behind them by hand. Around 90% were dedicated promotion accounts: every post about betting, the same templates, constant calls to move to WhatsApp or Telegram.

Most had between 1,000 and 5,000 followers and little genuine engagement. They look less like a community and more like a well-resourced, top-down distribution system.

Local “books” and exchanges that issue betting IDs

The names that come up most are not the international brands. They are local operators that call themselves a book or an exch. Here “book” is bookie slang, as in booking a bet: Samrat Online Book, Dhanwan Book, Everest Book. Exchanges like Thala7 Exch and Dynamo work the same way. You message an agent on WhatsApp and get a betting ID, an account to place bets with. Some take their names from cricket fandom, like Thala7 and Virat 777.

Their posts follow a template: a fixture, two star players, “10% welcome bonus, 5% refill bonus,” “24x7 withdrawal,” a phone number. One brand under #dafabetcricket posted the same layout hundreds of times, changing only the teams.

Paid vs organicदो रास्ते, एक वादा

Two channels, one pitch

Side by side, the two channels share a core of easy money and safety, and differ in how they deliver it. Paid ads lead with features and deception. Organic posts carry what is too blunt for paid placement: manipulated success stories, addiction framed as fun, countdown clocks.

Google Play · 58 appsडाउनलोड के बाद

After the download

We collected the one-star reviews of 58 betting and casino-style apps that the ads and posts pointed to, about 3.3 lakh in all, and grouped them with topic modelling into ten themes. One-star reviews are not a survey of users. They are a large record of what people say when an app fails them, frustrates them or harms them.

The largest theme is money. Together with generic negative reactions and complaints about rigged play, nearly 60% of reviews describe loss, unfairness or deception.

What 329,501 one-star reviews are about

Consolidated topic-model themes. Select a theme to read reviews from it.

“They first let you win”

One pattern recurs across apps. Users win early, buy chips or make a deposit, and then lose fast. Many describe the odds as reacting to their spending, a kind of predatory difficulty adjustment designed to extract money.

Glitches follow the same shape in users’ telling. The app works for deposits, then crashes or freezes on a winning hand or a withdrawal. These read less like reports of bad luck and more like reports of fraud.

Summaryहमने क्या पाया

What we found

Finding 1

The pitch and the experience don’t match

Ads show small stakes turning into large wins and frame outcomes as knowable. Reviews describe losing, blocked withdrawals, unclear rules and absent support.

99.4% → 28.9%of ads sell easy money · of one-star reviews are about financial loss and scams
Finding 2

Enforcement lags, and transparency depends on where you live

Meta had taken down 20,591 ads by the time we collected the data, after they had run. Ads shown in India disclose no reach, spend or payer; similar ads in the EU must.

20,591ads already taken down at collection
Finding 3

AI is a force multiplier

Generative tools make fake news segments and celebrity endorsements cheap to produce, which fits a burn-and-churn playbook of short-lived pages and ads.

14.8%of paid ads used AI-generated or manipulated media
Finding 4

The shadow funnel survives takedowns

Ads and posts push users to Telegram, WhatsApp, look-alike sites and disguised app listings. Removing one app leaves the recruitment infrastructure on Meta intact.

8.1%of distinct ads linked straight to Telegram

Methodsहमने यह कैसे किया

How we did it

The study treats promotion and harm as linked stages of one pipeline, and follows the same apps and keywords through each source.

  1. Snowball the vocabularySeed keywords → Instagram hashtag searches → linked accounts, Telegram groups and sites → new app names and coded terms, until the list stopped growing.
    84 keywords
  2. Collect paid adsMeta Ad Library API, searched with the keyword list, covering 2023 to mid-2025. Creatives downloaded from each ad’s snapshot page.
    2023–2025
  3. Code every ad by handNear-identical images and videos grouped with PDQ and TMK+PDQF perceptual hashes, so each unique creative was coded once: category, app, messaging strategy, harmful narratives, media authenticity, sexual content, notes.
    10,526 ads
  4. Scale to InstagramGemini 2.5 Pro, few-shot with the codebook and labelled examples. 97% accuracy on 300 randomly sampled held-out posts.
    2,607 posts
  5. Read the reviewsOne-star Google Play reviews for the apps linked from ads and posts. BERTopic on sentence embeddings; topics labelled with an LLM and checked by hand on 50 reviews from each of 15 topics.
    220 topics → 10 themes
The cleanest tier of the marketReviews come only from Google Play, which polices real-money gambling more strictly than the open web. Sideloaded APKs and offshore sites are likely worse.
A conservative countOnly harms explicitly present in an ad’s text or visuals were coded, so subtler manipulation is likely undercounted.
What hashtags missHashtag discovery cannot see private accounts or Stories. The Instagram sample is for comparison, not prevalence.
Reviews are not a surveyOne-star reviews over-represent unhappy users and miss those who never write one. The analysis rests on recurring themes, not single complaints.
