A data investigation · India · 2023–2025

Win guaranteed. Withdrawal pending.

Illicit betting apps find Indian users through a flood of Facebook and Instagram ads that promise quick, risk-free money. We read 10,526 of those ads, 2,695 Instagram posts and 329,501 one-star app reviews to compare the pitch with what people say happens after they sign up.

Research by Aatman Vaidya (University of Tübingen) and Kiran Garimella (Rutgers University)

Online betting is now a multi-billion-dollar global industry. It sells sports bets, casino games, fantasy leagues and quick-fire prediction games, and cheap mobile data has put all of it in every pocket, at every hour.

In India the business sits in a legal grey zone. Gambling is regulated state by state. Many platforms describe themselves as “games of skill.” Offshore operators target Indian users from outside the country’s jurisdiction, and when they are blocked they return with new domains, new brand names and surrogate pages. News reports and public-health researchers have tied these apps to financial loss, debt, addiction and, in some cases, loss of life. A 2025 law aims to curb real-money gaming ads.

For most users, the first contact with a betting app is an ad in their feed: a short video, a celebrity, a screenshot of a winning slip. So this study starts with recruitment. What do the ads promise? How does that promise spread outside paid advertising? And how does it compare with what users report once they have downloaded the app and deposited their money?

What the ads say · 10,526 annotated ads

Nearly every ad sold easy money

We coded each ad for what it promotes, how it tries to persuade, and which potentially harmful narratives it carries. The codebook grew out of the ads themselves: one of us annotated an initial batch, and new categories were added as they appeared.

Two narratives ran through almost the entire corpus. The ads were also built for conversion. 99.8% were aimed at signing up new users, 85.9% sold a specific feature such as an instant withdrawal or a sign-up bonus, and 59.1% leaned on social proof: winning slips, testimonials and screenshots of payouts.

98.4%Risk minimization. Outcomes framed as safe, knowable or verified.

What the ads sold

Share of the 10,526 betting ads in each category. An ad can fall in more than one.

Casino games led, with slot machines spinning out jackpots and crash games climbing to 2.72x. Sports betting ads leaned on winning slips and “high-confidence” predictions across major and minor leagues.

Many casino-style ads never named an operator or a legal entity. They showed the win, the balance and the bonus (“200% welcome bonus”) and left out who was making the offer.

A fifth of the ads sold something else entirely.

Camouflage

Gambling dressed as finance

One in five betting ads, 20.1%, sold trading or crypto. Lottery and jackpot formats added another 18.9%. These ads borrow the look of fintech: dashboards, candlestick charts, trading bots, tables that convert an “investment amount” into a “daily income.”

The disguise widens the audience beyond people who think of themselves as gamblers, and makes it harder to recognise the creative as prohibited betting promotion.

On Instagram, where people searching betting hashtags already know what they are looking for, the camouflage almost disappears.

Synthetic credibility

Borrowed faces

Without a friend’s share or a creator’s following to vouch for them, paid ads manufacture trust. Fake news bulletins. Cloned voices. Celebrities who never said a word.

The game being sold

The plane always flies away

Aviator is a crash game. A multiplier climbs from 1.00x as a small plane takes off. Cash out before it flies away and your stake is multiplied. Wait too long and the stake is gone. Rounds last seconds, so a player can bet hundreds of times in an hour.

The ads sell it as something you can read. “Predictor” apps promise to call the crash from the strip of past results at the top of the screen. Telegram channels sell signals. A testimonial video shows a running profit counter.

Simulated rounds, drawn at random with a 3% house edge

Each round is an independent draw. The strip of past results that “predictor” apps claim to read carries no information about the next one.

Crash games advertise a 97% “return to player.” That means that across all bets, the house keeps about three rupees in every hundred. It does this every round, forever, whatever the player does.

One ad in our data turned the house’s cut into a slogan: “97% players win every day.”

Try every strategy

2.00xwins 48.5% of rounds
1,000 simulated players each start with ₹10,000 and bet ₹100 a round for 200 rounds, cashing out at the same target every time.

No cash-out target beats the 3% edge. Low targets win often and bleed slowly. High targets lose most rounds and occasionally pay out big. Those rare big wins are real, and they are exactly the screenshots that end up in ads and on Instagram.

This simulation is ours and not part of the study. It models a fair crash game with a 3% house edge. Users in the reviews below describe apps that behave worse than that.

Burn and churn

Here today, gone in four days

Betting ads lived fast. In the full collection, over 60% of ads ran for only one to three days. Among the betting ads we annotated that had a recorded end date, the median ran for four days, and two in three were gone within a week.

Short runs suit a simple playbook. Generate a creative, launch it from a fresh page, collect sign-ups for a few days, abandon the page before enforcement catches up. The ads we annotated came from 1,224 different Facebook pages. Generative AI has pushed the cost of a new creative close to zero.

Enforcement does happen, eventually: 20,591 ads in our collection had already been removed. But during the lag, the platform keeps the ad revenue and the advertiser keeps the sign-ups. Meta’s own policies prohibit real-money gambling ads without prior written permission, and prohibit deceptive practices.

There is also a transparency gap. For ads shown in India, the Ad Library gives no reach, spend, payer or beneficiary for this kind of ad. The same kind of ad shown in the European Union has to disclose who paid for it and who saw it.

Instagram · 10 hashtags · 2,695 posts

The organic layer speaks cricket

Hashtags show what circulates outside the ad system. We collected 2,695 public posts from ten betting hashtags. Gemini 2.5 Pro coded the 2,607 relevant ones, using our ad codebook and hand-labelled examples. On 300 held-out posts it matched our judgement 97% of the time.

The organic layer is blunter than the ads. 71.9% of posts were about sports betting, overwhelmingly cricket. It leans on celebrities, emotion and winners’ stories, and it says out loud what paid ads avoid.

Tap a post to see how it was coded. Posts are a random sample from each hashtag; posts flagged as sexual content are excluded.

A distribution network that looks like a community

We reviewed about 200 posts and the profiles behind them by hand. Around 90% were dedicated promotion accounts. Every post was about betting. The templates were consistent, and so were the calls to move to WhatsApp or Telegram.

Most of these accounts had between 1,000 and 5,000 followers and little genuine engagement. They look like a well-resourced, top-down distribution system dressed as fan pages.

Indian “books” and exchanges selling betting IDs

The names that come up most are not the international brands. They are Indian “books” and “exchanges” that sell betting IDs over WhatsApp: Thala7 Exch, Dynamo, Samrat Online Book, Dhanwan Book, Everest Book. Some take their names from cricket fandom, like Thala7 and Virat 777.

Their posts follow a template: a fixture, two star players, “10% welcome bonus, 5% refill bonus,” “24x7 withdrawal,” a phone number. One #dafabetcricket brand posted the same layout hundreds of times with only the teams changed.

Paid vs organic

Two channels, one pitch

Side by side, the two channels share a core of easy money and safety, and differ in how they deliver it. Paid ads lead with features and deception. Organic posts carry the content that is too blunt for paid placement: manipulated success stories, addiction framed as fun, countdown clocks.

Meta ads were annotated by hand. Instagram posts were annotated by Gemini 2.5 Pro with the same codebook. Some gaps, such as “promoting a new feature” against “general promotion,” partly reflect the two methods; the difference in tone held up on manual review.

Off-platform

The shadow funnel

Facebook and Instagram are rarely where money changes hands. They sit at the top of a funnel that pushes people somewhere less visible: a Telegram channel, a WhatsApp number, a throwaway website, an app listing that pretends to be something else.

“Mera loss recover kara do”

We sampled Telegram groups linked from the ads. Many are organised around one betting form, such as football tips, or around one platform. Tips are presented as near-certain, and uncertainty as something insider knowledge can solve.

Some ads go further and sell recovery from losses. One creative in the corpus is a screenshot of a direct message: a user who has lost ₹95,000 begs for help, and the promoter offers to “cover” it for a 40% cut of the withdrawal. Another ad, in Hindi, offers to cover your losses with “ten minutes of hacking” for 30% of the winnings.

This is why taking down a single app achieves little. If Google Play removes a listing, the recruitment machinery on Meta stays in place and points to a new domain or a new channel.

Google Play · 58 apps · 329,501 one-star reviews

After the download

We collected the one-star reviews of 58 betting and casino-style apps that the ads and posts pointed to, and grouped them with topic modelling. BERTopic found 220 topics, which we consolidated into ten themes. One-star reviews are not a survey of users. They are a large record of what people say when an app fails them, frustrates them or harms them.

The largest theme is money. Combined with generic negative reactions and complaints about rigged play, nearly 60% of reviews describe loss, unfairness or deception.

What 329,501 one-star reviews are about

Consolidated BERTopic themes. Select a theme to read reviews from it.

“They first let you win”

One pattern recurs across apps. Users win early, buy chips or make a deposit, and then lose fast. Many describe the odds as reacting to their spending, a kind of predatory difficulty adjustment designed to extract money.

Glitches follow the same shape in users’ telling. The app works for deposits, then crashes or freezes on a winning hand or a withdrawal. These read less like reports of bad luck and more like reports of fraud.

Summary

What we found

Finding 1

The pitch and the experience don’t match

Ads show small stakes turning into large wins and frame outcomes as knowable. Reviews describe losing, blocked withdrawals, unclear rules and absent support.

99.4% → 28.9%of ads sell easy money · of one-star reviews are about financial loss and scams
Finding 2

Enforcement lags, and transparency depends on where you live

Meta had removed 20,591 ads by the time we collected them, after they had run. Ads shown in India disclose no reach, spend or payer; similar ads in the EU must.

20,591ads already removed at collection
Finding 3

AI is a force multiplier

Generative tools make fake news segments and celebrity endorsements cheap to produce, which fits a burn-and-churn playbook of short-lived pages and ads.

14.8%of paid ads used AI-generated or manipulated media
Finding 4

The shadow funnel survives takedowns

Ads and posts push users to Telegram, WhatsApp, look-alike sites and disguised app listings. Removing one app leaves the recruitment infrastructure on Meta intact.

8.1%of distinct ads linked straight to Telegram

Methods

How we did it

The study treats promotion and harm as linked, measurable stages of one pipeline, and follows the same apps and keywords through each source.

  1. Snowball the vocabularySeed keywords → Instagram hashtag searches → linked accounts, Telegram groups and sites → new app names and coded terms, until the list stopped growing.
    84 keywords
  2. Collect paid adsMeta Ad Library API, queried in date batches back to 2023, collected mid-2025. Creatives downloaded with Selenium from each ad’s snapshot page.
    ≈340,000 → 23,620
  3. De-duplicatePDQ and TMK+PDQF perceptual hashes at 95% similarity grouped near-identical images and videos; labels propagated across each group.
    9,892 unique
  4. Annotate by handInductive coding by one author over every unique creative: relevance, category, app, messaging strategy, harmful narratives, media authenticity, sexual content, notes.
    10,526 betting ads
  5. Scale to InstagramGemini 2.5 Pro, few-shot with the codebook and labelled examples. 97% accuracy on 300 randomly sampled held-out posts.
    2,607 posts
  6. Read the reviewsOne-star Google Play reviews scraped for the apps linked from ads and posts. BERTopic on all-MiniLM-L12-v2 embeddings (UMAP, HDBSCAN); topics labelled with Qwen2.5-7B and checked by hand on 50 reviews from each of 15 topics.
    220 topics → 10 themes
The cleanest tier of the marketReviews come only from Google Play, which polices real-money gambling more strictly than the open web. Sideloaded APKs and offshore sites are likely worse.
A conservative countOnly harms explicitly present in an ad’s text or visuals were coded, so subtler manipulation is likely undercounted.
What hashtags missHashtag discovery cannot see private accounts or Stories. The Instagram sample is for comparison, not prevalence.
Reviews are not a surveyOne-star reviews over-represent unhappy users and miss those who never write one. The analysis rests on recurring themes, not single complaints.
